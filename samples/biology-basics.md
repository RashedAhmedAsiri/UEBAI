# Unit 1: The Cell

## Cell Structure

All living things are made of cells. The cell is the smallest unit of life that can carry out all life processes. Robert Hooke first described cells in 1665 when he looked at cork under a microscope.

Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane, a thin flexible barrier that controls what enters and leaves the cell. Inside the membrane is the cytoplasm, a jelly-like fluid where many chemical reactions happen.

Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus that holds the cell's DNA. Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, have no nucleus; their DNA floats in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid.

Mitochondria are the powerhouses of the cell. They release energy from food molecules through cellular respiration and produce ATP, the cell's energy currency.

Plant cells also have a rigid cell wall made of cellulose, a large central vacuole that stores water, and chloroplasts where photosynthesis takes place.

## Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the process plants, algae and some bacteria use to turn light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It takes place mainly in the leaves, inside chloroplasts.

The overall equation is: carbon dioxide + water, using light energy, produces glucose + oxygen. In symbols: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2.

Chlorophyll is the green pigment that absorbs light, mostly red and blue wavelengths. Green light is reflected, which is why leaves look green.

The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes and produce ATP and NADPH, releasing oxygen from water. The Calvin cycle happens in the stroma and uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugar.

Factors that limit the rate of photosynthesis include light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature.

# Unit 2: Animal Diversity

## Mammals

Mammals are warm-blooded vertebrates. They have hair or fur, and females produce milk from mammary glands to feed their young.

Most mammals give birth to live young. Placental mammals, like humans and whales, nourish the embryo through a placenta. Marsupials, like kangaroos, give birth to tiny young that develop in a pouch.

Monotremes are unusual mammals that lay eggs. The platypus and the echidna are the only living monotremes, found in Australia and New Guinea.

Mammals have a four-chambered heart, which keeps oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood separate. This supports their high metabolism and constant body temperature.

Mammals have specialized teeth: incisors for cutting, canines for tearing, and molars for grinding food.

## Birds

Birds are warm-blooded vertebrates with feathers, beaks and no teeth. All birds lay hard-shelled eggs.

Most birds can fly. Their bones are hollow and light, and powerful chest muscles attach to a large breastbone called the keel.

Birds have a very efficient respiratory system with air sacs, so air flows through the lungs in one direction.
